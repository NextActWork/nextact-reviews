What your offer letter doesn’t say

NextActs follows the labor market in aggregate — claims, payrolls, the unemployment rate. This does the opposite: it reads the single document at the center of one person’s move, and tells you what’s in it and when the clocks run out.

Got a PDF? Open it, press ⌘A / Ctrl A to select all, ⌘C / Ctrl C to copy, then paste here. If the PDF is a scan there’s no text to copy — paste the email the offer arrived in instead.

🔒 Processed on this device. The letter is read by code running in your browser. It is never uploaded, never logged, and nothing is kept when you close this tab. If you want to check that claim rather than take our word for it: load this page, disconnect from the network, and it still works.
What we do count: anonymous event names — parsed, ics, or which field you corrected. That name is the entire message; no text from your letter, no IP, no cookies, no tracking. We count it because this is a beta and we need to know whether it works.

Why this exists. We built it against real offer letters, and the most useful thing we learned was how little they commit to. Of the small set we tested, most expressed the response deadline as an interval — “valid for three (3) working days from the date of this letter” — rather than naming a date. None of them stated a start date; that was a blank line, “a mutually agreed date”, or a form field.

It’s a handful of letters, not a survey — but it was enough to change what we built. The deadline you’re working to often isn’t written down anywhere. It has to be counted. That’s the job this does.

      What this can’t do yet

      It is a beta, and the honest version of that word: accuracy is not independently validated, it abstains where the wording is ambiguous rather than guessing, and any deadline it calculates is an estimate worth confirming.

      Every limit, in full
      • Accuracy is not independently validated. It’s been built and checked against a small number of real letters and a synthetic test set we wrote ourselves. That catches regressions; it does not prove it generalises to your letter.
      • It abstains a lot, on purpose. Naming the wrong deadline is far worse than naming none, so where the wording is ambiguous it leaves the field blank and says so.
      • Calculated deadlines are estimates. When a letter says “three working days”, we count weekdays — we don’t know your employer’s holiday calendar, and whether the count includes the letter’s own date is genuinely ambiguous. Confirm anything we compute.
      • Scanned PDFs won’t work. There’s no text to read in an image. Paste the email instead.
      • It doesn’t tell you whether your offer is good. That needs market data this tool doesn’t have. It reports what the document says and what it leaves out.

      Not legal or tax advice. It reports the contents of a document you already have, and flags dates worth confirming with the people who sent it.
